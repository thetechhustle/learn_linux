# 30.3 Cooling and Environment

!!! abstract "What you will learn"
    - Explain why cooling, airflow, humidity, dust, and water risk matter to Linux service reliability.
    - Collect safe host-side evidence when a server may be thermally stressed.
    - Write a clear remote-hands or facilities handoff for a cooling or environmental concern.

!!! example "Field story"
    A batch job slows down every afternoon. CPU graphs show reduced throughput, but nothing obvious changed in the application. A data center technician later reports that the rack sits near a failed floor tile damper and several servers are pulling warm exhaust air back into their front intakes.

!!! success "Operator principle"
    Cooling is not just a facilities detail. Heat, blocked airflow, water, dust, and humidity can turn a healthy Linux service into an intermittent incident.

## Why Linux operators care

Linux operators usually do not design the mechanical system for a data center, but they are often the first people to notice thermal trouble through symptoms:

- sudden hardware sensor alerts
- CPU throttling or lower-than-normal throughput
- unexpected fan speed increases
- disk, power supply, or memory errors that cluster around heat events
- recurring service incidents tied to a room, rack, row, or time of day

The job is to connect host evidence with physical context. A good operator can say, "These three hosts in rack B17 began reporting high inlet temperature at 14:10, and the affected services recovered after airflow was restored." That is more useful than saying, "The servers seem hot."

## Common environmental risks

Cooling and environment work includes more than room temperature.

| Risk | Why it matters | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High inlet temperature | Servers may throttle, fail components, or shut down | BMC/iDRAC/iLO sensor readings, hardware logs, fan speed |
| Hot-air recirculation | Exhaust air returns to the front intake of servers | Rack position, blanking panels, cable blockage, nearby exhaust |
| Blocked airflow | Cables, boxes, missing panels, or dense gear disrupt cooling | Photos from remote hands, rack inspection, affected host list |
| Humidity too low | Static discharge risk increases | Facilities readings, ESD incidents, room environmental alarms |
| Humidity too high | Condensation and corrosion risk increase | Facilities readings, water leak alarms, visible moisture |
| Dust and debris | Fans and heat sinks lose efficiency | Photos, maintenance history, fan alerts |
| Water exposure | Cooling leaks or roof leaks can damage equipment | Leak sensors, photos, affected rack and floor location |

## Airflow basics

Most data centers separate cold supply air from hot exhaust air.

- **Cold aisle:** the front side of racks where server intakes face the conditioned air.
- **Hot aisle:** the back side of racks where servers exhaust warm air.
- **Blanking panels:** covers for unused rack spaces that stop hot air from looping through empty gaps.
- **Cable management:** keeps power and network cables from blocking rear exhaust.
- **Containment:** doors, panels, or curtains that keep hot and cold air streams separated.

Do not assume a room is fine because one wall thermostat looks normal. A single rack can be in trouble if airflow is blocked or if hot exhaust air is returning to the front of the servers.

## Safe Linux-side checks

Use read-only checks first. Not every host exposes every sensor from the operating system, and many server-grade sensors are better read through the baseboard management controller.

```bash
# Kernel messages that may mention thermal, CPU, memory, disk, or hardware faults
journalctl -k --since "2 hours ago" | grep -Ei 'thermal|temperature|throttl|mce|edac|fan|power|hardware'

# CPU frequency and possible throttling context
lscpu
grep -H . /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu*/cpufreq/scaling_cur_freq 2>/dev/null | head

# Local sensor output if lm-sensors is installed and configured
sensors

# Recent hardware-related service logs on systemd hosts
journalctl --since "2 hours ago" | grep -Ei 'thermal|temperature|fan|ipmi|bmc|mce|edac'
```

For physical servers, the best evidence often comes from the management controller:

```bash
# Examples only: command names vary by vendor and site tooling
ipmitool sensor list
ipmitool sel list
racadm getsensorinfo
```

!!! warning "Do not make cooling incidents worse"
    Avoid stress tests, benchmark loops, firmware updates, or unnecessary reboots on hardware that may already be overheating. Preserve evidence, reduce nonessential load if your runbook allows it, and coordinate with facilities or remote hands.

## What to ask remote hands or facilities

When you cannot see the rack, ask for specific observations.

```text
Cooling/environment check request

Location:
- Site:
- Room/row/rack:
- Affected hosts:

Symptoms:
- First observed time:
- Host-side evidence:
- Services affected:

Please check:
- Are front intakes facing the cold aisle?
- Are blanking panels missing near the affected hosts?
- Are cables or objects blocking front intake or rear exhaust?
- Are nearby floor tiles, vents, or containment doors out of place?
- Are fans unusually loud or showing amber LEDs?
- Are there visible water, dust, or debris issues?
- What are the local temperature and humidity readings near the rack?

Requested evidence:
- Photo of rack front
- Photo of rack rear
- Any facility alarm or sensor reading near the rack
```

## Troubleshooting pattern

1. Confirm the scope: one host, one rack, one row, one room, or one service tier.
2. Capture host evidence before making changes.
3. Check whether symptoms correlate with time of day, workload, rack position, or facility alarms.
4. Reduce risk: pause nonessential batch jobs or move traffic only if the service runbook allows it.
5. Ask remote hands for physical evidence, not just "looks fine."
6. Record the fix and verify recovery with the same metrics used to detect the problem.

## Lab: build a cooling incident note

Use a lab VM or a non-production host. You do not need to create a real heat problem.

1. Run `journalctl -k --since "2 hours ago"` and save any hardware-related lines.
2. Run `lscpu` and save CPU model and frequency information.
3. If `sensors` exists, record the output. If it does not, write down that the host lacks OS-visible sensor data.
4. Draft a remote-hands request using the template above.
5. Add a short verification plan: what metric or log would prove the issue is resolved?
